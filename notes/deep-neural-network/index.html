Step 1 · lines 79-84

Words become a row of lights

A neural network can only do arithmetic. It can't read. So the first puzzle is how to turn a paragraph into a fixed-length list of numbers.

Ed's answer is one line: HashingVectorizer(n_features=5000, stop_words="english", binary=True). It does four things to every product summary:

  1. Lowercase everything and cut the text into words of two or more letters or digits.
  2. Drop 318 very common stop words such as "and", "for" and "with".
  3. Run each remaining word through a hash function, a fixed recipe that turns any word into a big number, and keep the remainder after dividing by 5,000. That gives every word a bucket from 0 to 4,999, and the same word always lands in the same bucket.
  4. Light up those buckets. The other buckets stay at 0.

Notice that every lit bucket holds the same value. binary=True sets each one to 1, then sklearn quietly divides the whole list by its length (its default L2 normalization). With 34 lit buckets, each one holds 1/√34 = 0.1715. Longer descriptions spread the same total over more buckets.

Three consequences, decided before any learning happens:

  • Word order is gone. "noise cancelling headphones" and "headphones cancelling noise" light exactly the same cells. This is called a bag of words.
  • Some words share a bucket. 5,000 buckets isn't many. In just 150 products from the course data, 389 buckets already hold two or more different words: "iphone" shares with "ideal", "ukulele" with "raspberry". Across 800,000 products every bucket is crowded, so the network can only learn what a bucket means on average.
  • Nothing is learned here. The hash recipe is fixed, so it needs no training data and works the same on products it has never seen. That's why line 83 (fit_transform) and line 89 (transform) behave identically.

A quirk worth knowing: sklearn's stop list also throws away some useful product words, including "front", "side", "top", "full" and "system". A "front bumper" arrives as just "bumper".

Show the answer

Those buckets belong to "title", "category", "brand", "description" and "details", the labels that start each line of every summary. A cell that's lit for every product can't tell any two products apart, so training learns to give it little weight. It's the hashing version of background noise.

Step 2 · lines 85-99

Prices become friendly numbers

Next we choose exactly what the network should guess. Raw dollars are a poor target. In a sample of 150 products from the course data, prices run from $8.08 to $849.99. A $10 miss on an $8 calendar is a disaster, while a $10 miss on an $850 guitar is excellent, yet in dollars both count the same, and the expensive products dominate the learning.

Ed reshapes the target in two moves:

  1. Take the log, log(price + 1). Multiplying a price by 10 now just adds 2.3, so $8, $80 and $800 become evenly spaced at 2.2, 4.4 and 6.7. The +1 keeps a $0 price safe, because log(0) is undefined.
  2. Standardize: subtract the average log price, then divide by the spread (the standard deviation). A typical product now sits at 0, and most fall between −2 and +2. Networks learn fastest when their targets live in that range.

Here the average (4.451) and spread (1.012) come from those 150 products. Ed's code computes the same two numbers from all 800,000 training products in lines 96-97, so his exact values differ a little.

Pause and ponder

The network outputs −2.2 for some product. Roughly what price is it predicting?

Show the answer

Run the two moves backwards: −2.2 × 1.012 + 4.451 = 2.22, then e2.22 − 1 ≈ $8.2. About the price of the hockey calendar.

Step 3 · lines 31-41

One neuron is a weighted vote

Now 5,000 numbers go in, almost all of them 0. The first thing they meet is nn.Linear(5000, 4096) on line 37: a layer of 4,096 neurons.

A neuron is simple. It multiplies every input by its own weight, adds up the results, and adds one more number called the bias. A positive weight means "this input pushes me up", a negative one means "this input pushes me down". Training is nothing more than finding good weights.

Here's the shortcut worth remembering. Since nearly every input is 0, a neuron just adds up the weights of the words that are present.

Zoom out to all 4,096 neurons and each bucket effectively owns a column of 4,096 weights, one per neuron. A product's first-layer output is the sum of its words' columns. Training shapes those columns so that words signalling "expensive" end up pushing in similar directions. That's 5,000 × 4,096 = 20,480,000 weights before the network has even started thinking.

Pause and ponder

This neuron has +0.9 for "wireless" and −0.9 for "calendar". Which product makes it fire hardest, and what has it become?

Show the answer

One with many of the positive words and none of the negative ones, like the headphones. It has become a small "premium electronics" detector. Real neurons are rarely this tidy. Most respond to blends of many words, which is why we judge a network by its results rather than by reading neurons one at a time.

Step 4 · lines 36-41

Three small tools after every sum

The raw sums never go straight to the next layer. Line 37's Linear is followed by three small tools, and the same pattern repeats inside every block later on. Step through them and watch what each one does to the numbers.

  • LayerNorm keeps every layer's numbers in a familiar range, so a change in the early layers doesn't knock the later ones off balance. It's a big reason a 10-layer network trains smoothly.
  • ReLU is the bend. Without it, stacking Linear layers would be no more powerful than a single one, because a straight-line function of a straight-line function is still a straight line.
  • Dropout is a training exercise. Any neuron might vanish on any step, so the network can't lean on a few of them, and it learns patterns that hold up on products it has never seen.
Pause and ponder

Why does the code switch dropout off with model.eval() before predicting?

Show the answer

Dropout is practice with a handicap. When predicting you want the whole network's steady answer. With dropout still on, asking about the same product twice would give two different prices.

Step 5 · lines 11-28 and 43-57

Residual blocks add a correction and keep the original

Ed wants depth: num_layers=10. That's one input layer, then num_layers - 2 = 8 residual blocks (line 45), then one output layer. Deep stacks of plain layers are hard to train, because the learning signal fades as it travels back through layer after layer.

Each block is a mini network of six pieces you've already met. The trick is line 27:

The block doesn't replace its input. It computes a correction F(x) and adds it to the original. If the best move is to change nothing, the block only has to learn to output zeros, which is easy.

Why this matters, in one line of calculus: the slope of F(x) + x is F′(x) + 1. That + 1 is a highway. However small F′ gets, the learning signal has a route back to the early layers where nothing is lost. Toggle it below.

Pause and ponder

Suppose a block learns nothing useful and F(x) is all zeros. What comes out of the block?

Show the answer

ReLU(0 + x) = ReLU(x), and since the x coming in has already been through a ReLU, it has no negatives, so it passes through unchanged. A useless block costs compute, not accuracy, which is what makes it safe to stack many of them.

pricer/deep_neural_network.pylines 11-28
class ResidualBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_size, dropout_prob):
        super(ResidualBlock, self).__init__()
        self.block = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(hidden_size, hidden_size),
            nn.LayerNorm(hidden_size),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Dropout(dropout_prob),
            nn.Linear(hidden_size, hidden_size),
            nn.LayerNorm(hidden_size),
        )
        self.relu = nn.ReLU()

    def forward(self, x):
        residual = x
        out = self.block(x)
        out += residual  # Skip connection
        return self.relu(out)
pricer/deep_neural_network.pylines 43-57
        # Residual blocks
        self.residual_blocks = nn.ModuleList()

        x = self.input_layer(x)

Step 6 · lines 49 and 172-175

From 4,096 numbers back to a price

After the last block, nn.Linear(4096, 1) on line 49 takes one final weighted vote over the 4,096 numbers and produces a single number: the predicted z-score of the log price from Step 2. To turn it into dollars, line 173 runs Step 2 backwards.

Pause and ponder

Why does line 175 wrap the answer in max(0, result)?

Show the answer

The output layer can produce any number at all. If it predicts a z so low that the log price comes out below 0, then e(that) − 1 is slightly negative, and a negative price makes no sense. max(0, …) clips it to $0.

pricer/deep_neural_network.pylines 167-175
    def inference(self, item):
        self.model.eval()
        with torch.no_grad():
            vector = self.vectorizer.transform([item.summary])
            vector = torch.FloatTensor(vector.toarray()).to(self.device)
            pred = self.model(vector)[0]
            result = torch.exp(pred * self.y_std + self.y_mean) - 1
            result = result.item()
        return max(0, result)

Step 7 · lines 101-103

Where 289 million numbers live

Line 103 prints the size of the network: 289,128,449 parameters. Every one is a weight or a bias that training adjusts. Here's where they live, and what happens when you change the two size knobs from line 32.

  • Input layer: 5,000 × 4,096 weights + 4,096 biases + 8,192 LayerNorm values = 20,492,288 (7%).
  • Each residual block: two 4,096 × 4,096 grids of 16,777,216 weights, plus biases and LayerNorm values = 33,579,008. Eight blocks make 268,632,064 (93%).
  • Output layer: 4,096 weights + 1 bias = 4,097.

Each parameter is a 32-bit float, 4 bytes, so the saved weights file is about 1.16 GB. Training needs about four times that, roughly 4.6 GB, because it also keeps a gradient and two AdamW running averages for every parameter.

Pause and ponder

Halve hidden_size to 2,048. Do you get half the parameters? Predict, then try it with the slider.

Show the answer

No. The blocks shrink to about a quarter, because each block's grids are hidden_size × hidden_size. The total drops from 289 million to about 77 million. Width is the expensive knob; depth grows the count in a straight line.

Step 8 · lines 105-140 and 158

Learning means nudging every number downhill

So far the 289 million numbers are random, and the guesses are nonsense. Learning happens in a loop, and it needs three ingredients: a score for how wrong a guess is, a way to find which direction makes it less wrong, and a step size.

How wrong is a guess?

Line 115 picks nn.L1Loss(): the average of |prediction − truth|, measured in z-units. Why absolute error instead of the more common squared error? Drag the dots.

The single guess that makes the total absolute error smallest is the median. Squared error picks the mean, and one expensive outlier can drag the mean anywhere. There's a bonus: the log and the standardizing from Step 2 never change which price is in the middle, so predicting the median z is the same as predicting the median price. And the median is exactly the guess that minimizes average dollar error, the number Ed's evaluator reports.

One step of learning

Each pass around the loop handles 64 products (line 120), shuffled into a new order every epoch. With 800,000 products that's 12,500 steps per epoch, and Ed runs 5 epochs: 62,500 steps, about 4 hours on his M1 Mac's GPU, the mps device that lines 105-110 pick when there's no NVIDIA card.

The learning rate is the step size

The gradient says which way is downhill. The learning rate says how far to step. Here's the same idea with a made-up model that has just one number to learn, so we can see the whole valley.

AdamW (line 116) starts from lr=0.001 and adapts the step for each number individually, taking bigger steps where the slope has been consistent. weight_decay=0.01 also pulls every weight slightly towards 0 on each step, another guard against memorizing. Line 137 caps the total step at length 1.0 (gradient clipping), so one strange batch can't fling the weights off course.

Shrinking the steps over time

Line 117's cosine schedule lowers the learning rate along a smooth curve: big steps early to make progress, small steps late to settle into a good spot. It's updated once per epoch (line 158).

Letting the rate wind down often gives a slightly better final model. It hasn't been tested on this one, so treat it as an idea to try, not a known fix.

Pause and ponder

Why reshuffle the products every epoch (shuffle=True on line 120)?

Show the answer

If batches always arrived in the same order, say all the car parts together, each step would pull the weights towards one kind of product and the next would pull them back. Shuffling makes every batch a fair mini-sample of all 800,000 products, so each nudge points in a more trustworthy direction.

    def train(self, epochs=5):
        for epoch in range(1, epochs + 1):
            self.model.train()
            train_losses = []

            for batch_X, batch_y in tqdm(self.train_loader):
                batch_X = batch_X.to(self.device)
                batch_y = batch_y.to(self.device)

                self.optimizer.zero_grad()
                outputs = self.model(batch_X)
                loss = self.loss_function(outputs, batch_y)
                loss.backward()

                # Gradient clipping
                torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(self.model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)

                self.optimizer.step()
                train_losses.append(loss.item())

Step 9 · lines 142-158

Checking honestly after every epoch

Getting better on the training products isn't the goal. Getting better on products it has never seen is. So after every epoch the code switches to exam mode:

  • model.eval() turns dropout off.
  • torch.no_grad() skips the gradient bookkeeping, since nothing will be updated.
  • It predicts 1,000 validation products that were held out of training (val[:1000] in Ed's notebook) and scores them with the same L1 loss.
  • Line 149 converts the predictions back to dollars, so the printout also shows a number a human can feel.
Pause and ponder

After epoch 1 the validation loss (0.4324) is lower than the training loss (0.5463). Shouldn't a network do better on the data it trained on?

Show the answer

Two effects. The training loss is averaged over the whole epoch, including the early steps when the network was still poor, while validation is measured at the end. And during training, dropout handicaps the network by silencing 20% of its neurons; validation runs at full strength.

            # Validation
            self.model.eval()

                # Convert back to original scale for meaningful metrics
                val_outputs_orig = torch.exp(val_outputs * self.y_std + self.y_mean) - 1
                mae = torch.abs(val_outputs_orig - self.y_val.to(self.device)).mean()

            avg_train_loss = np.mean(train_losses)
            print(f"Epoch [{epoch}/{epochs}]")

Once trained, the weights are saved to a file (save, line 161, about 1.16 GB) and loaded later (load, line 164). map_location lets weights trained on Ed's Mac GPU load onto a machine with only a CPU. Then inference runs everything from Steps 1 to 6 in one pass.

A product mentions a word that never appeared in the 800,000 training summaries. What does the network do with it?

Show the answer

The hash still sends it to some bucket, and that bucket's weights were trained on whichever other words share it. So the new word borrows the average meaning of its bucket-mates. Hashing never fails on an unknown word, but it can quietly mislead.

Was it worth it?

Ed built this network to, in his words, "redeem ourselves" after fine-tuning GPT-4.1-nano disappointed. Here's how everything in Week 6 scored on the same 200 test products, as average dollar error. Shorter bars are better.

A network trained from scratch, with no idea what headphones are, nearly matched GPT-5.1 and beat the fine-tuned GPT-4.1-nano by about $29 per product. With 800,000 labelled examples of one narrow task, a purpose-built model can stand toe to toe with the frontier.

Four things a careful reader notices

  1. Memory. Line 84 turns the 800,000 × 5,000 sparse matrix into a dense one: 32 GB as 64-bit floats, plus a 16 GB 32-bit copy, about 48 GB at peak. That's far more memory than a typical laptop has. The fix is to keep the matrix sparse and convert one batch at a time.
  2. The schedule never winds down. T_max=10 with 5 epochs leaves the learning rate at 65% (Step 8).
  3. "binary" isn't 0 and 1. The default L2 normalization makes each lit bucket 1/√k (Step 1). Adding norm=None would give true 0/1 values.
  4. 5,000 buckets collide a lot. 389 shared buckets in just 150 products (Step 1). With sparse input you could afford far more buckets.

Can you explain these without scrolling up?

Why predict a z-score of log(price + 1) instead of dollars?

So a $10 miss on a cheap item counts for more than on an expensive one, and so the targets sit near 0, where training is easiest. Line 173 undoes it at the end.

Why L1 loss instead of squared error?

Absolute error is minimized by the median, which outliers can't drag around. The median z maps to the median price, and the median price is the guess that minimizes average dollar error, the metric being reported.

What does out += residual buy you?

Each block learns a correction instead of a replacement, and the + x gives the learning signal a lossless route back through all 8 blocks.

Where do the 289 million parameters come from?

Mostly from sixteen 4,096 × 4,096 weight grids, two per residual block, 268.6 million in all. The input layer adds 20.5 million.

Why could this beat a fine-tuned GPT-4.1-nano?

It trained on 800,000 examples instead of at most 20,000, every one of its parameters works on this one task, and it outputs a number directly with a loss that matches the metric. A language model writes the price as text tokens, where "99" and "100" look nothing alike.

How the numbers were made. Step 1 runs the same recipe as sklearn's HashingVectorizer (MurmurHash3 into 5,000 buckets), checked against sklearn on 150 products from the course data, with identical buckets and values. The price average and spread come from those same 150 products. The training printout and the results chart come from Ed's notebooks. The weights in Steps 3 and 4 and the 55% in Step 5 are illustrations, because the trained weights aren't published with the course.
